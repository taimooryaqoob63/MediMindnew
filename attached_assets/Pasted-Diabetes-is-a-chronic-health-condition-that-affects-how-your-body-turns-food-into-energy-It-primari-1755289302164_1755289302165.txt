Diabetes is a chronic health condition that affects how your body turns food into energy. It primarily involves the hormone insulin, which is produced by the pancreas. Insulin helps glucose from food get into your cells to be used for energy. When you have diabetes, your body either doesn’t make enough insulin or can’t use the insulin it makes as well as it should. This leads to elevated levels of glucose in the blood, known as hyperglycemia, which can cause various health issues over time. There are two main types of diabetes: 1. Type 1 Diabetes: This is an autoimmune condition where the body's immune system attacks and destroys the insulin-producing cells in the pancreas. People with type 1 diabetes need to take insulin every day to manage their blood glucose levels. Type 2 Diabetes: This is the more common form of diabetes and is often associated with lifestyle factors such as obesity and physical inactivity. In type 2 diabetes, the body becomes resistant to insulin or the pancreas fails to produce enough insulin. It can often be managed with lifestyle changes, oral medications, and sometimes insulin. According to the NHS, managing diabetes involves monitoring blood glucose levels, maintaining a healthy diet, engaging in regular physical activity, and, if necessary, taking medication or insulin therapy. The NICE guidelines emphasize the importance of individualized care plans to manage diabetes effectively, which include regular monitoring and adjustments to treatment as needed to prevent complications such as cardiovascular disease, nerve damage, and kidney problems. For healthcare professionals, understanding diabetes care involves recognizing the symptoms, which can include increased thirst, frequent urination, extreme fatigue, and blurred vision, and knowing how to support patients in managing their condition through education and appropriate medical interventions.
